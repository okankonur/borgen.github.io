The most popular films made from the *Odyssey* and the *Iliad* came roughly 2,750 years after the epics were first committed to writing: Wolfgang Petersen's *Troy* in 2004 and Christopher Nolan's *Odyssey* in 2026. [The Iliad covers the last 50 days of the Trojan War and ends with Hector's funeral. The film overlaps a little with the Odyssey.]{.marginnote} As a film, I love *Troy*. I think it was a very successful epic Hollywood blockbuster. Looking back now, it failed to recoup its production cost domestically at the box office, but made three times that worldwide.[**Source:** Budget and box office figures for *Troy* (2004): Box Office Mojo — roughly $175m budget, $133m in the US and Canada, $497m worldwide.]{.marginnote} When I hear "the Trojan War," scenes from that film are what come to my mind too. Yet until the last couple of years, the only thing in my head about the historical reality of it was: "a war fought in Çanakkale in antiquity, Homer wrote about it, it became an epic." But every stone you turn over on this subject reveals another research topic underneath: from the story of how the epic reached us, to how the mounds called *höyük* form, to the perspective on the past held by the people we call ancient, to where geological change on the earth's surface sits on the scale of human history.

The Trojan War holds an important place in Anatolia's history too. A few events in the past have had the phrase "Troy has been avenged" attributed to them:

### 1. Mehmed the Conqueror (1462) — the only sourced example

This is the strongest and genuinely documented example. During the 1462 Lesbos campaign, Mehmed the Conqueror visits the ruins of Troy (Ilion), asks after the tombs of Achilles and Ajax, praises the heroes, and speaks to this effect:

> "It was to me that fell the lot, after so long a time, of avenging this city and its people. For it was the Greeks, Macedonians, Thessalians and Peloponnesians who laid it waste; and their descendants have now, through me, paid the penalty for the injustice they did to us Asians, then and in the times that followed."[**Source:** Michael Kritovoulos of Imbros, *History of Mehmed the Conqueror*, book IV. Greek text: *Critobuli Imbriotae Historiae*, ed. Diether Roderich Reinsch, Corpus Fontium Historiae Byzantinae 22 (Berlin: De Gruyter, 1983); English translation by Charles T. Riggs (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1954). Kritovoulos was an Ottoman bureaucrat of Greek origin who dedicated his work directly to Mehmed — that is, a contemporary source close to the court. The claim that Mehmed framed the 1453 conquest of Constantinople the same way is generally derived from this passage.]{.marginnote}

### 2. The saying attributed to Atatürk — very widespread, but unsourced

After the 1922 Great Offensive and the entry into İzmir, Atatürk is said to have declared **"We have avenged the Trojans."** But no primary source for this can be produced. No such statement appears in *Nutuk* or in the collected *Speeches and Statements of Atatürk*; the story is generally repeated second- or third-hand, without a citation.[**Where it isn't:** Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, *Nutuk* (Ankara, 1927), and *Atatürk'ün Söylev ve Demeçleri* (Speeches and Statements of Atatürk), 3 vols. (Ankara: Atatürk Research Centre). The phrase appears in neither of these two core corpora, and no publication has yet traced it to a primary record.]{.marginnote}

# The Epic and the City

The Trojan War is fascinating in two respects. The first is the road these epics — written about the war and its aftermath — travelled to reach us. The second, and the central example of the question of time scale I'll try to touch on in this piece, is the story of the city of Troy itself.

## How the Epic Reached Us
The first interesting point. The war takes place toward the end of the Bronze Age, somewhere around 1150–1200 BC. When Homer^[Incidentally, it isn't even clear whether Homer was one person or a group of bards.] as master bard dictated it into writing, the year was around 700 BC. So for the roughly 450 years in between, this story was passed down entirely orally, from bard to bard, generation to generation. The reason it was written down in Homer's time is that the Phoenician alphabet emerged in that period. Once that alphabet spread across the Mediterranean, they used it to record the epic while they had the chance. [Now you'll ask: what about cuneiform, the Sumerian script? That was used for the Epic of Gilgamesh, and why the West never took it as the foundation of its own culture the way it took the Iliad — despite it being older — is another subject entirely.]{.marginnote} By the time it was written down, 450 years had passed since the Trojan War. It's rather like an event from the 1600s being written down for the first time now.[**Source:** On the technique of oral composition, Milman Parry, *The Making of Homeric Verse*, ed. Adam Parry (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1971), and Albert B. Lord, *The Singer of Tales* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1960). On the arrival of the alphabet and the writing down of the epic, Barry B. Powell, *Homer and the Origin of the Greek Alphabet* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1991); on the dating, M. L. West, "The Date of the Iliad," *Museum Helveticum* 52 (1995): 203–219.]{.marginnote} For Homer, Achilles was 15 generations back. And consider: Ancient Greece — the age of Socrates, Plato, Aristotle — would come 400 years *after* Homer had this written down. These famous philosophers, ancient from where we stand, would look at the Iliad from the same vantage point we do: an epic out of the very distant past. Four hundred years later, Aristotle would praise Homer in the *Poetics* for choosing a single portion of the war rather than narrating all of it.[**Source:** Aristotle, *Poetics* 23, 1459a30–37.]{.marginnote}

The epic's own journey through the world went like this:

1. Before writing, it was carried for centuries by **oral tradition**.
2. **Homer** had it written down around 700 BC.
3. **Athens**, in the 500s BC, made official written copies mandatory so that bards would not corrupt the story.
4. Scholars at the **Library of Alexandria** edited the texts and divided each into the 24 "books" we still use today.
5. The **Byzantines** hand-copied the crumbling papyrus onto durable parchment throughout the Middle Ages.
6. After the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, Greek scholars fled to Italy with their manuscripts, and it was printed for the first time **in Florence in 1488**.[**Source:** On the history of the text, M. L. West, *Studies in the Text and Transmission of the Iliad* (Munich: K. G. Saur, 2001), and Gregory Nagy, *Poetry as Performance: Homer and Beyond* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996). The official Athenian copy (the "Peisistratean recension") rests on ancient reports and is disputed today: Cicero, *De oratore* III.137; Pausanias VII.26.13. Editio princeps: *Homeri Opera*, ed. Demetrios Chalkondyles (Florence, 1488).]{.marginnote}

### The City

The Bronze Age Hittites kept records. Their tablets speak of a region in western Anatolia called Taruisa/Taruwisa and of a city there named *Wilusa*. This resembles the Greek *Troia* and *Ilios* (Ilion). The Iliad takes its name from the latter. The tablets also mention a people called *Ahhiyawa*; these are almost certainly the *Achaeans*, that is, the Greeks.^[One Hittite treaty gives the name of the king of Troy as *Alaksandu* — startlingly close to *Alexandros*, Prince Paris's other name in the *Iliad*.] The Trojans were not exactly Hittites; they were the **Luwians**, a western Anatolian people, close cultural cousins from the same Indo-European family.[**Source:** For the Hittite documents, Gary Beckman, *Hittite Diplomatic Texts*, 2nd ed. (Atlanta: Scholars Press, 1999), no. 13 (the Alaksandu treaty, CTH 76); Gary Beckman, Trevor Bryce and Eric H. Cline, *The Ahhiyawa Texts*, Writings from the Ancient World 28 (Atlanta: Society of Biblical Literature, 2011); Trevor Bryce, *The Trojans and Their Neighbours* (London: Routledge, 2006). For the Luwians, H. Craig Melchert, ed., *The Luwians* (Leiden: Brill, 2003).]{.marginnote}

The city sits in a critical position, at the mouth of the strait where ships waited for the northeasterly to pass, and so it grew rich as time went on; that is also why the Mycenaeans, the ancestors of the ancient Greeks, attacked it. Although the legendary cause of the Trojan War in the epic is Helen, the real cause was gaining control of this harbour.

And here is the part I find most incredible: **the Trojan War is as distant from us as the first settlement on their own hill was from the people living in Ilion at the time of the Trojan War.**


## Ten Cities Stacked on the Same Hill

Historically it has generally been assumed that the Trojan War really happened but that its mythological elements were legend. Where exactly the city of Ilion mentioned in the war stood, however, could not be proven. The argument ran on and on through history. Finally, in the 1870s, a German businessman and treasure hunter named **Heinrich Schliemann** came to the Çanakkale area and started digging into a low hill called Hisarlık. With hundreds of workers he cut a vast trench down through the middle of the mound, and out came walled cities piled one on top of another, burned again and again, rebuilt again and again.[**Source:** Heinrich Schliemann, *Ilios: The City and Country of the Trojans* (London: John Murray, 1880) and *Troja* (London: John Murray, 1884). For a critical assessment of his methods and claims, David A. Traill, *Schliemann of Troy: Treasure and Deceit* (London: John Murray, 1995). For Frank Calvert, who pointed at the hill before him, Susan Heuck Allen, *Finding the Walls of Troy: Frank Calvert and Heinrich Schliemann at Hisarlik* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1999).]{.marginnote}

Listing these layers from a distance like this is easy. But think about the time within each layer in terms of a single life. *Each layer holds generations, grandfather to grandchild*; a person who spent an entire life inside Troy VIIa experienced it as *static*, permanent, the only world there was. Childhoods, meals, arguments, harvests, decades of ordinary life. **And today, from where we stand, that whole vast lifetime is compressed into a thin band of ash a centimetre thick.**

The hill the city stands on holds roughly 4,500 years of unbroken settlement. And when Mehmed the Conqueror arrived there in 1462, not even two centuries had passed since the last settlement.

So Troy was not a "lost city" — it lived on for another 2,500 years after Homer's war. In the Hellenistic-Roman period it was Ilium Novum — a place with a temple of Athena Ilias, a theatre, walls, a place Rome granted privileges to as "the city of our ancestors." And it was a regular pilgrimage stop:

- Xerxes (480 BC) called in before crossing to Greece and sacrificed to Athena.
- Alexander (334 BC), the moment he set foot in Asia, went there, poured oil on Achilles' tomb and ran naked, and took armour from the temple.
- Caesar, Augustus and Caracalla stopped by; Caracalla staged a Patroclus-imitating funeral there for his friend Festus.
- Julian (~AD 354) describes his visit in one of his own letters: bishop Pegasios guided him, and the altar at Hector's tomb was still warm.[**Sources:** Xerxes: Herodotus, *Histories* VII.43. Alexander: Arrian, *Anabasis* I.11.5–12.1; Plutarch, *Alexander* 15. Fimbria's sack of 85 BC: Appian, *Mithridatic Wars* 53; Strabo, *Geography* XIII.1.27. Caracalla and Festus: Herodian IV.8.3–5; Cassius Dio LXXVIII.16.7. Julian: *Letters* 79, ed. and trans. W. C. Wright, Loeb Classical Library (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1923). For the visits taken together, Rose, *The Archaeology of Greek and Roman Troy* (2014).]{.marginnote}

So in the 15th century Troy was not a "lost city." It was a ruin whose location was known, whose stone was visible above ground, whose name had been carried unbroken since Homer. For someone living in Troy VIIa, Troy I was already an antiquity *1,800* years old; for Alexander, Troy VIIa was 1,000 years away; for Julian, Alexander's visit was 700 years earlier. Every generation standing on that same hill looked back at the ones before it the way we look back at all of them.

## If the Name Ilion Was Known All Along, Why Was Its Link to Troy So Hard to Find?

For centuries the ruins lay beneath a city that carried their own name. There are several reasons they went unseen:

### 1. Antiquity had already rejected the site
This is the biggest reason. In the 2nd century BC, Demetrios of Skepsis, a scholar from the region, argued that "the city now calling itself Ilion is not Homer's Troy," and placed the real site a few kilometres away. **Strabo** adopted this view and took it into his *Geography*. Strabo was Europe's number one authority on ancient geography right up to the 19th century. So the most widely read source flatly declared the correct address to be the wrong one.[**Source:** Strabo, *Geography* XIII.1.25–43; trans. H. L. Jones, Loeb Classical Library, vol. VI (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1929). Demetrios of Skepsis's thirty-book commentary *On the Trojan Catalogue* is lost; his views are known only through Strabo's report. For a modern assessment of the controversy, J. M. Cook, *The Troad: An Archaeological and Topographical Study* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1973).]{.marginnote}

### 2. The name had been forgotten
When Byzantine Ilion gradually broke up toward the 13th century, no living settlement carrying that name remained. In the Ottoman period the hill was called **Hisarlık**; meaning "place of fortresses," a name shared by dozens of spots across Anatolia. A traveller arriving with Homer in hand would have been looking at a field nobody called "Ilion."

### 3. For eighty years the wrong hill was assumed
In the 1780s the French traveller Jean-Baptiste Lechevalier proposed that the real Troy was **Pınarbaşı (Balı Dağ)**, 10 km to the south. His argument: in the Iliad, Achilles chases Hector around the city's two springs, one hot and one cold; Pınarbaşı has abundant springs, Hisarlık does not. This view prevailed through most of the 19th century.[**Source:** Jean-Baptiste LeChevalier, *Description of the Plain of Troy*, trans. Andrew Dalzel (Edinburgh, 1791); expanded as *Voyage de la Troade*, 3 vols. (Paris, 1802). For the 19th-century debate over the site, Cook, *The Troad* (1973), and Allen, *Finding the Walls of Troy* (1999).]{.marginnote}

### 4. The hill didn't look like anything
Hisarlık was a low, cultivated hummock; the stones visible on top were late Roman remains, giving the impression of an ordinary provincial town. It occurred to nobody that 15 metres of accumulated city layers might lie beneath it; neither the concept of the **tell** nor the method of reading stratification was yet a standard tool of archaeology. On top of that, the geography didn't fit: Homer's Troy is on the seashore, whereas Hisarlık today is 6 km inland. That the Karamenderes and the Dümrek had filled the bay with alluvium over three thousand years was only understood later.

### 5. Nobody had dug
For centuries the argument was conducted entirely by reading texts and walking the terrain.

![Hisarlık, a low hummock barely distinguishable from the plain around it. Beneath today's visitor path lies fifteen metres of accumulated city.](/pics/hisarlik-tepesi.jpg){.fullwidth}


## Same Hill, Same Wind, Same Birds, Same Sunset

The most extraordinary thing to contemplate when you visit Troy is that the wind blowing there is **exactly the same wind**. Homer calls Ilion "windy" more than a dozen times.[The present-day name for this relentless north wind is **poyraz**; it funnels down from the Black Sea into the Dardanelles.]{.marginnote} Sailing ships cannot head straight into the wind; boats wanting to work their way up the strait had to anchor in the bay below and wait for the wind to turn, sometimes for weeks. While they waited, they paid for water, food and harbour dues. That is what made Troy rich.[**Source:** On the wind, the current and the city's position, Manfred Korfmann, "Troy: Topography and Navigation," in *Troy and the Trojan War*, ed. Machteld J. Mellink (Bryn Mawr: Bryn Mawr College, 1986), 1–16. For the objection to reading Troy as a trading and harbour city, Frank Kolb, "Troy VI: A Trading Center and Commercial City?" *American Journal of Archaeology* 108 (2004): 577–613.]{.marginnote} So the air that tosses your hair when you stand in the ruins today is the same air that hummed in Hector's ears as he looked down from the wall.

But there is something odd here. According to the Iliad, the walls are counted as within walking distance of the ships. In the film *Troy*, too, they were shown as walkable, if not exactly close. If the city's reason for existing was the wind and the harbour, and the wind is still there — where is the harbour? When you stand at Hisarlık and look out, you see no sea at all; you see flat fields, and the coast is 5–6 kilometres away.

**The only thing that changed was the geography.** Troy once stood on the shore of a deep bay. Two rivers descending from Mount Ida[Homer's **Ida**. The two rivers coming down from it: the **Karamenderes** (the epic's *Scamander*) and the **Dümrek** (*Simoeis*).]{.marginnote} carried alluvium for three thousand years; the bay first grew shallow, then became marsh, then dry plain.

![The bay first grew shallow, then became marsh, then dry plain. The one thing that doesn't change is the wind crossing all three panels.](/pics/truva-kiyi-cizgisi.en.svg){.fullwidth}

At the time of the war the bay had partly silted up, but it was still a sheltered anchorage a few kilometres across. That must be where the Greeks drew their ships ashore. For a long time this was taken as proof that Homer had made everything up: the poet says soldiers went back and forth between the ships and the walls several times in a single day, which would have been impossible if the coast had been where it is now. Then, in the 1990s, geologists took deep cores all across the plain and brought up sea fossils, salt-marsh clay and beach sand from beneath the wheat fields. Around 1250 BC a bay really did reach far inland — right up to the spot where Homer places the Greek camp.[**Source:** John C. Kraft, İlhan Kayan and Oğuz Erol, "Geomorphic Reconstructions in the Environs of Ancient Troy," *Science* 209, no. 4458 (1980): 776–782; John C. Kraft, George Rapp, İlhan Kayan and John V. Luce, "Harbor Areas at Ancient Troy: Sedimentology and Geomorphology Complement Homer's Iliad," *Geology* 31, no. 2 (2003): 163–166. The core series were published alongside the excavation in İlhan Kayan's reports in *Studia Troica*.]{.marginnote} The coastline and the geography were not like that in the period when Homer set it down in writing, which means this doesn't show he invented it; on the contrary, it shows that a war fought 500 years earlier really was passed on by word of mouth across generations with these details intact. It's an incredible thing. If you claim Homer made it up — could he have invented this geographical difference that was only proven later?

## The Rivers
Homer says the Scamander's name among the gods is *Xanthos*, that is, "yellow." The water coming down from Mount Ida still runs the same yellow-brown today: because of the alluvium it carries. So the colour of the river and the material that buried the city are exactly the same thing. In book 21 of the Iliad this river, as a god, floods over Achilles, and the hero barely saves his life by clinging to an elm on the bank.[The trees on the bank are the ones in the epic too: willow, tamarisk, elm.]{.marginnote} The Karamenderes still has the same temperament; it swings between a thin thread you can step over in summer and a flood that covers the plain in winter and spring. The **Bayramiç Dam**, completed in 1996, now holds it back upstream: the river that wrestled with Achilles today heads a network irrigating fifteen thousand hectares on the plain. In book 12 the gods join these very two rivers, the Scamander and the Simoeis, and sweep away the wall protecting the Greek ship camp — and indeed not a single stone of that wall has ever been found. The plain swallowed everything.[**Source:** *Iliad* XX.73–74 (the name Xanthos), XXI.233–382 (the Scamander flooding over Achilles), XII.17–33 (the gods joining the two rivers and sweeping away the Greek wall). English text: Homer, *The Iliad*, trans. Richmond Lattimore (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1951).]{.marginnote}

## The Two Springs
In book 22, as Achilles chases Hector around the walls, they pass two springs: one steaming hot, the other as cold as if it were full of ice even in midsummer; and beside them the stone troughs where the women of Troy washed clothes before the war. This single detail shifted the search, for most of the 19th century, to Pınarbaşı 10 km to the south, because there is no such hot-cold pair around Hisarlık. But in 1997 Korfmann's team found something else south of the citadel: a **cave and tunnel system** cut into the rock, carrying water from underground. Uranium-thorium dating of the stalactites on its ceiling showed that the system came into use around 2900 BC — that is, in the very first centuries of Troy I — and continued in use into the Roman period.^[In the 2000s the plain's springs were also surveyed hydrogeologically and hydrochemically; Homer's pair of springs still has not been definitively matched.] Groundwater holds a constant temperature year-round, and its mouth steams on a cold morning. On top of that, the Alaksandu treaty on the Hittite tablets counts an underground watercourse among the sacred witnesses of Wilusa. So beneath the city there is a water source that was cut two thousand years before the epic was written down, and was held sacred.[**Source:** The two springs: *Iliad* XXII.147–156. The cave and tunnel system south of the citadel, and the uranium-thorium dating of its stalactites: Manfred Korfmann, "Troia — Ausgrabungen 1997," *Studia Troica* 8 (1998). For the underground watercourse (KASKAL.KUR) among the divine witnesses of Wilusa, Beckman, *Hittite Diplomatic Texts*, no. 13.]{.marginnote}

## The Shore Where the Ships Were Drawn Up
The epic places the Greek camp between two headlands: *Sigeion* and *Rhoiteion*. Both are still in place. One is the ridge on the Kumkale-Yeniköy side, the other is **İntepe** at the far end of the plain. The bay between them is farmland now, but because the two capes are still visible you can trace the camp's boundaries with your eye. There is one alternative location as well: **Beşik Bay**, *outside* the strait, facing the Aegean.[The poyraz is one reason ships waited in harbour; the other is the current: a surface current runs constantly down the strait from the Marmara toward the Aegean, reaching several knots at the narrows. Under sail you cannot beat against both the wind and the water.]{.marginnote} The 1982-87 excavations turned up a Bronze Age cemetery at the edge of the bay: a community buried with Mycenaean pottery, with women, men and children but no elderly. In other words, sailors who died there while waiting for the wind. Beşik is a beach today.[**Source:** Manfred Korfmann's preliminary reports on the Beşik-Tepe excavations, *Archäologischer Anzeiger* (1984–1988); final publication of the cemetery: Maureen A. Basedow, *Beşik-Tepe: Das spätbronzezeitliche Gräberfeld*, Studia Troica Monographien 1 (Mainz: Philipp von Zabern, 2000).]{.marginnote}

## The Conical Mounds on the Plain
Standing in the middle of the fields and along the coastal ridges are conical mounds you can pick out from a distance: Sivritepe, Beşik Sivritepe, İntepe, Üvecik Tepe. Throughout antiquity these were pointed out as the tombs of the heroes; Sivritepe was held to be **Achilles'** tomb, and another one overlooking the strait **Ajax's**. These are the tombs Alexander poured oil on, the ones Mehmed asked the whereabouts of in 1462. They are still there today, and you drive right past them. The strange thing is this: not one of the excavated ones produced a Bronze Age warrior. The largest of them, Üvecik Tepe, turned out to belong to Festus, the friend for whom Caracalla staged the Patroclus-imitating funeral. So the mounds track not the epic but the epic's admirers: the plain accumulated not only layers of city, but layers of **pilgrimage**.[**Source:** For the excavation and identification of the tumuli on the plain, C. Brian Rose, *The Archaeology of Greek and Roman Troy* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2014), and J. M. Cook, *The Troad* (1973). Caracalla's funeral for Festus: Herodian IV.8.4–5; Cassius Dio LXXVIII.16.7.]{.marginnote}

## Where Poseidon Sat
In book 13, Poseidon watches the war "from the highest peak of wooded Samothrace"; from there, we are told, he sees Ida, Ilion and the Greek ships all at once. Standing on the Trojan plain and looking out, you cannot see Samothrace directly; Gökçeada stands in front of it. But Samothrace's 1,600-metre Fengari summit is visible over the ridge of Gökçeada in clear weather.[This is one of the reasons travellers coming to Troy since the 19th century have thought "Homer really did know this place."]{.marginnote} So the poet seated his god at the one point from which the whole scene is visible at once. Only someone who had stood there and looked at the horizon could pick that point.[**Source:** *Iliad* XIII.10–14. On the visibility of Samothrace over the ridge of Gökçeada, Walter Leaf, *Troy: A Study in Homeric Geography* (1912), and Cook, *The Troad* (1973).]{.marginnote}

## Almost Everything Else Stayed the Same:

- Ida, which Homer calls "many-fountained, mother of wild beasts," still smells of red pine, thyme, marjoram and sage in the summer sun.
- In book 3 of the Iliad the clamour of the Trojan army is likened to "the cry of cranes." That coast is a major migration route; storks and cranes still fly over the ruins every spring and autumn.
- Bozcaada[Its ancient name is **Tenedos**. It still stands on the horizon at dusk, where Achilles looked.]{.marginnote} still stands on the horizon; the sun still falls into the Aegean and throws the same long golden shadows over the limestone and the yellow summer grass.
- When Homer describes the weak point of the wall, he gives the wild fig tree there (*erineos*) as the address; Andromache tells Hector to hold that spot. Wild figs still grow on the slopes of the mound.[**Source:** Ida as "many-fountained, mother of wild beasts": *Iliad* VIII.47 and XIV.283. The simile of the cranes: III.2–7. The wild fig tree (*erineos*): VI.433–439 and XXII.145.]{.marginnote}
- Flatbread baked on a hot stone, olive oil, salted sheep's cheese, figs, heavy stews with chickpeas and broad beans, red wine cut with water against the midday heat. You could set the same table today.
